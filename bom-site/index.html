The design I would build

Four independently driven 80 mm mecanum wheels on the four owned NEMA 17 steppers, a low 350 × 390 mm 2020 frame, the battery slung under the deck for a low centre of gravity, the Ceres pan/tilt turret at the front carrying your webcam and a thermal array so it can look around and track, and both radars on a 200 mm post at the rear looking forward over the turret. Two short-range ToF sensors guard front and rear. The fifth motor and TB6600 stay spare.

No kit does this. Every mecanum chassis kit on Amazon is built for TT or DC gearmotors; none has a NEMA 17 mount. Steel L-brackets under the rails are the cleanest way to put your steppers on mecanum wheels, and the 5 mm couplers in the wheel set match the 5 mm flatted shaft on your motors.

Power is one HOOVO 3S 2200 mAh shorty LiPo for the motors and servos — through one of your XL4015 5 A bucks for the 6 V servo rail — and a small USB power bank for the Pi, which keeps the computer isolated from motor-bus noise. The bank's mass is the price: a compact 5,000 mAh unit fits with the ramp margin at 1.28; a 300 g brick does not fit at any current.

Finding people is a four-sensor job, and that is how professional search-and-rescue kit is built: CO2 to narrow the area, thermal to confirm a body, a microphone for voice and knocking, radar for behind obstacles. The rover had only the radar leg. So the pan/tilt turret now carries a thermal array beside the camera — a warm body is unmistakable in 768 pixels, in the dark or in smoke, and thermal-plus-visible on one sweep is the strongest line-of-sight detector on the vehicle. A CO2 sensor rides the radar post for closed rooms, and an I2S microphone listens when the motors are stopped. The Pi runs MobileNet-SSD person detection on the camera at roughly 10 frames per second (TFLite at 300 px on a Pi 4; a Coral stick would triple it); fusing that with the thermal blob is the visible-track demo. The hidden track is the radar's breathing-like periodicity plus WiFi CSI — and the word for it is periodicity, not life.

LD2410C is the primary presence sensor for anyone the camera cannot see: stationary or micro-moving people behind a door or a stud wall, plus breathing. LD2450 adds positions and speeds of moving targets and loses motionless bodies beyond about 2 m; it is not the primary detector for an immobile person. Neither outputs heartbeat measurements — and the 60 GHz breathing/heartbeat modules on the market were skipped: their heart-rate figure benchmarks at a 0.33 correlation against a chest strap, and the owned LD2410C already covers breathing. Start with one radar powered; test the two radar types separately, then together for interference. These sensors are not validated here for finding a motionless person behind concrete, rubble, fire or smoke. No detection must never be interpreted as proof that nobody is present. One more constraint the geometry forces: the turret sits 270 mm ahead of and 60 mm below the radar boresight — inside the beam. Read the radars only with the turret parked and the motors stopped, and discard LD2450 targets closer than 0.5 m; otherwise every pan registers as a moving person. After the rover stops, wait about five seconds before trusting the LD2410C — it needs a still platform to settle. And configure it: the LD2410C's gates 0–1 (0–1.5 m) look straight at the turret's metal servo and camera clamp and will report a stationary person forever; set those two gates' sensitivity to 100 (ignore) with the HLK config command — Walleye's ld2410.py already speaks it.

Power and connections

  • HOOVO T-plug -> Deans pigtail (half of an OliRC extension) -> two 5-way Wago 221-415 (one per polarity) -> three 22 AWG branches: shield VMOT (four DRV8825 -> four motors), XL4015 IN (6 V servo rail), LM2596 IN (5 V fan + radars). 22 AWG is rated 7 A against a 4 A burst. No fuse, by your call — the T-plug is the disconnect, so bring the mating pair UP through the rear deck slot and mount it on top at the rear edge: the emergency stop must be reachable one-handed from above, not under a deck. A fuse and in-run cell monitoring were declined and are listed under Safety as the reviewer's recommendation.
  • Power-up ORDER: bank → Pi → R4 first, THEN the pack. Power-down in reverse. With the R4 unpowered the DRV8825 enable pins float low — enabled — so VMOT alone puts full holding current into four motors with nothing to stop it.
  • Power bank -> Pi USB-C. The bank must hold 5 V at 3 A: a 2.4 A bank browns out the moment the camera, thermal array and ESP32 are all drawing. Pi USB-A -> UNO R4 USB-C for logic power and serial. Fallback if the bank misbehaves: an XL4015 or LM2596 trimmed to 5.10 V on PHYSICAL HEADER PINS 2 or 4 (5 V) and 6 (GND) — physical pin numbers, not BCM GPIO numbers — and never with the bank connected at the same time; two supplies in parallel fight.
  • XL4015 OUT -> PCA9685 V+ terminal. Set to 6.0 V with a multimeter before connecting. Its 5 A rating covers the tilt servo stalling (~2.5 A) with room to spare now that the locked pan servo is disconnected; an LM2596 would sag there. Servo current returns through the PCA9685 terminal, never through a USB ground.
  • Fan and both radars -> an LM2596 trimmed to 5.0 V from the pack, through two 5-way Wagos, not the Pi's USB. Pi USB carries only the webcam (~500 mA at 1080p), ESP32 #1 (~150 mA, WiFi OFF in firmware) and the R4 (~150 mA, radio off): ≈ 800 mA average, 1.05 A peak, inside a Pi 4's 1.2 A. Nothing CSI is wired on the rover. On a Pi 5 the default 600 mA USB cap must be lifted in config.txt.
  • Pi -> camera by USB. Pi -> ESP32-S3 by USB-C; the ESP32 bridges both radar UARTs. Radars take 5 V power and 3.3 V UART signalling.
  • Two VL53L1X share I2C only after XSHUT (BCM GPIO 22 and 27) assigns unique addresses at boot. Power the modules from the Pi's 3.3 V pin, never 5 V: their XSHUT and I2C pull-ups follow VIN, and a 5 V pull-up on a Pi GPIO damages it. PCA9685 is on the same bus at 0x40. If the optional SCD41 is fitted, this bus runs at 100 kHz — that sensor's I2C maximum.
  • Single common ground at the shield GND terminal. Never put LiPo voltage on a logic rail.

Motors at 1.5 A per phase draw about 2 A from the pack at cruise — copper loss dominates, not motion — plus up to ~0.8 A average for the servos. One 2200 mAh HOOVO gives roughly 35–40 minutes (the second pack in the box doubles that) of driving; check the pack with the multimeter before and after every run and stop at 11.1 V — there is no alarm on the balance lead. Holding current at rest costs the same as moving, so cut ENABLE (D8) when parked; mecanum wheels on a level floor do not roll away.

The owned three-pin switch is not in the load path until its DC rating is read off the body and is at least 10 A.

One star ground at the shield GND terminal. The Pi sits on its own bank and reaches that ground two ways — through the R4's USB cable and through the PCA9685's logic GND pin — so keep the PCA9685 power return heavy and short, or servo current will find the USB cable and corrupt the serial link. That exact fault took down the Ceres rover once.

Unplug the T-plug when the rover is parked for more than a few minutes. Cutting ENABLE stops the motor current, but the shield, XL4015 and LM2596 idle at tens of milliamps and will take the pack below 3.0 V/cell overnight, which ends it.

Ground clearance is 18.9 mm at the motor bodies. A door threshold or rug edge above ~15 mm stops this rover; plan the demo route around them — the 80 mm mecanum wheels climb about 10 mm.

Grounds, honestly: the USB cables to the R4 and ESP32 #1 are parallel ground paths whether we like it or not. Motor and servo return currents must go straight to the Wago negative bus — never through a board's GND pin — so those USB grounds carry only signal reference.

Motor and frame fit

Frame: two side rails along X with outer faces at Y = ±195; two cross rails along Y butted between them with outer faces at X = ±175. Rails sit at Z 71–91. FOUR corner brackets, one per joint on the two inner faces — a 2020 inner corner has room for exactly one 17 × 20 bracket, and a second on the top faces would sit under the deck. The deck tiles, bolted to all four rails, are the second shear plane. M5 T-nuts at 3.0 N·m maximum or the slot deforms. Measure the four rails first: the longer pair become the side rails, because a cross rail even 1 mm over 350 will not fit between them.

Drive: each steel L-bracket goes inverted under a side rail near its end, foot against the rail underside on two M4 T-nuts, leg hanging down flush with the rail's outer face. The motor bolts to the inboard side of the leg with four M3×6 at 0.8 N·m (aluminium threads — the full 1.1 N·m an M3 can take will strip the flange). Shaft through the leg, coupler on the flat with blue threadlocker, wheel on the coupler. Axle at X = ±125, Z = 40. Motor body bottom at Z = 18.9 mm: that is your ground clearance.

Mecanum handedness: front-left and rear-right take the left-hand wheels, front-right and rear-left the right-hand. Check from above — the rollers on top of each wheel should point toward the chassis centre, forming an X.

Deck: four printed quadrant tiles 170 × 195 × 5 — a full-width 390 mm tile fits no common print bed — each bolted to one side rail (3× M5) and one cross rail (2× M5), lapped at the centreline seams with 15 mm tongues and M3×8 + nuts, held by the corner kit's M5×8 screws into M5 T-nuts — the screw holes are counterbored so only 3 mm of tile sits under each head and the 8 mm screw still gets 5 mm of T-nut. Every board sits on 10 mm bosses printed into the tile and is held by cable ties through printed slots (the Pi and PCA9685 take M2 screws, their holes being 2.7 mm). Battery cradle hangs under the deck centre on four M3×12 with nuts from the M3 kit — 179 g is nothing for four M3s — with a printed retaining bar and two cable ties instead of a strap; battery bottom at Z 65. R4/shield stack rear-left, Pi rear-right, XL4015 ahead of the R4, PCA9685 beside the Pi, ESP32 rear-centre, power bank centred over the cradle. Turret base at X = +110 on four M3×16, pan stage locked. Wheels sit 5 mm outboard of the bracket legs — every millimetre of overhang multiplies the motor-bearing load. Radar post on two M4×12 into M4 T-nuts in the rear rail, flange and tile counterbored so the 12 mm screw reaches. Thermal array in a printed saddle on the camera clamp. Every screw on the rover comes from the two kits already on the list. Four PLA quadrants lapped and screwed are not one plate: the corner brackets carry squareness, the deck adds stiffness that has not been quantified, and PLA creeps under the turret's bolts over weeks — retorque before the demo.

Compute before claiming it works

Load cases at the design mass, 80 mm wheels, mecanum roller factor √2, mechanical efficiency 0.85, carpet rolling coefficient 0.12: level cruise, level at 0.3 m/s², strafing, and a 5° carpet ramp at 0.3 m/s². The ramp governs. Available torque is holding torque × (I / 1.7 A) × 0.70, where 0.70 is a conservative low-speed pull-out factor that has not been measured. Full arithmetic, every input tagged measured / allocated / assumed, is in drivetrain.txt; every placement is in assembly.csv.

At 1/16 microstepping (one MS3 cap per driver), 0.1 m/s is about 1,270 pulses/s per wheel and 0.3 m/s about 3,820 — 15,300 pulses/s across four axes, inside a 20 kHz timer ISR on the R4. Custom four-axis firmware on the UNO R4 with a timer interrupt, acceleration limits, shared enable and a communication timeout; GRBL is unsuitable and would not run on the R4 anyway. Motor order at the shield: X = FL, Y = FR, Z = RL, A = RR with the D12/D13 jumpers fitted.

Set DRV8825 current with Vref = 5 × Rsense × I. With 0.100 Ω sense resistors, 1.5 A is 0.75 V; read the actual resistor first. Start at 1.5 A with heatsinks and the fan; 1.7 A is the reserve, not the default.

Before calling it working: caliper every part marked VERIFY before drilling the deck; measure pull-out torque at 24 rpm and 1.5 A on the real battery with a lever arm and the hanging scale; then all four wheel directions raised; loaded forward, reverse, strafe, turn; watchdog stop; temperature after 10 minutes; radar trials one at a time then together; turret tracking with the camera fitted; marked-route obstacle stops. None of these tests has been performed.

Multimodal detection — four physics, one answer

Four ways of seeing a person, four different physics: the radar sees RF REFLECTED off a torso (range and periodic motion, through drywall); the ESP32 pair sees RF TRANSMITTED across a space and disturbed by a body (presence, through drywall, no range); the thermal array sees EMITTED heat (a warm blob, line of sight); the camera sees VISIBLE shape (a person-shaped box, line of sight). They fail differently, which is the point — but they also cannot all corroborate the same target: nothing optical sees through the wall.

So there are TWO TRACKS, never mixed. HIDDEN track (RF only): radar + CSI. VISIBLE track (optical only): camera + thermal, associated by position — a thermal blob and a person box count together only when they overlap in the image. A person the camera does not see is NOT vetoed by the camera; a radiator in this room and radar activity behind the wall are two observations, not one.

What the RF track can honestly claim: the radar reports periodic motion in the 0.13–0.60 Hz band with a confidence score; that score is a measure of REPETITION, not of life. Tested: a 0.35 Hz sine through the same code reads 21 breaths/min at 7.95/10. A fan, a curtain or a vibrating duct does the same. The label is therefore BREATHING-LIKE PERIODICITY, never ALIVE, and it needs: a still platform (windows during any rover or servo motion are discarded), timestamped samples (the tracker currently assumes 10 Hz), the same rate in two consecutive 30 s windows, and a range that stays put. Radar and CSI agreeing raises it to RF ACTIVITY, CORROBORATED — still not a person.

The CSI geometry, corrected by reading the firmware: the RuView nodes are RECEIVERS of the access point's frames, so the rover's own Pi hotspot is the transmitter and ESP32 #2 and #3 sit off the rover — one at the doorway the rover came through, one against the far wall of the search area — each on a USB charger, streaming CSI back over WiFi as UDP. Every rover position gives two RF paths through walls to two fixed points. CSI needs an empty-room calibration for the geometry it is in; without one it reports change, not presence.

The station protocol: stop; motors off; turret parked; 5 s radar settle; 30 s dwell running Walleye's vitals.py while the receiver logs CSI; then a rotation in 20° steps for the thermal + camera sweep and a coarse radar bearing — coarse because step counts give COMMANDED heading, and mecanum wheels slip; then report all four bars with their labels. About 60 s per station.

The sentence that survives a hard question: 'radar and WiFi both showed breathing-band periodicity 2 m behind that wall, stable for a minute; nothing optical could see it; go look.' Not 'the rover found a living person.'

Build order

  1. Caliper every part marked VERIFY: bracket foot and slots, wheel width, coupler length, webcam mass, board hole patterns, Ceres base holes. Adjust the tile files before printing anything.
  2. Print: four deck quadrants (220 mm bed), battery cradle and retaining bar, radar post FLAT as a U-channel plus its bar (200 mm as drawn; anything taller has to be printed in segments — a 256 mm bed's diagonal is 362 mm), fan bridge in PETG (the heatsinks below it reach 60 °C and PLA softens there), two ToF clips, thermal saddle (or skip it and VHB the array to the tilt bracket beside the clamp), pan lock. PLA for everything else. Counterbores as drawn. Start printing the day the order goes in: four tiles plus cradle, post, bridge and clips is roughly 20 hours of printer time. Before anything else, open every sensor box: MLX90640, VL53L1X, INMP441 and SCD41 breakouts routinely ship with header strips loose in the bag — if yours do, the soldering iron stops being optional.
  3. Frame. FIRST slide every T-nut into its slot — the kits' nuts are sliding, not drop-in, and a butt-jointed frame closes every slot end: per side rail 4 in the bottom slot (two motor brackets) and 6 in the top slot (deck); per cross rail 4 in the top slot (deck), 2 more in the rear one (post), and 1 in each outer face (ToF clips). Then butt the cross rails between the side rails, outer faces at X ±175 and Y ±195, one corner bracket per joint on the inner faces, 3.0 N·m. Check square with a tape across both diagonals — equal within 1 mm.
  4. Drive: four steel brackets inverted under the side rails, feet on two M4 T-nuts each at 2.0 N·m, legs flush with the outer faces, axle holes at X ±125. Motors to the legs with the four M3×6 that ship with the brackets, 0.8 N·m, leads inboard. Couplers on the flats with blue threadlocker. Wheels: diagonal pairs match (FL = RR, FR = RL); confirm by the drive-forward test, not by the diagram. Coupler: if its bore is a D, the flat depth must match the shaft's; if it is round with a grub screw, threadlocker is not optional.
  5. Shield: MS3 cap ONLY (DRV8825 1/16 — MS1 and MS2 stay open) under each of the four sockets with the drivers OUT. Drivers in, EN to EN. Jumpers on D12 and D13 of the A header. EN/GND open. Then, WITH VMOT CONNECTED and no motors plugged in, trim each Vref to 0.75 V (1.5 A at 0.100 Ω — read your sense resistor first) using a plastic trimmer tool: a metal screwdriver slipping off the pot onto VMOT kills the driver, and Vref reads nonsense with only USB power. Never plug or unplug a motor while VMOT is live. Then a 10 kΩ resistor from the EN pin to the 5 V header pin, so the drivers stay OFF while the R4 is in reset.
  6. Turret base on the front tile, M3×16. Camera into the Ceres clamp, thermal saddle on top. Radar post through the rear tile into the rail, bar on, LD2410C at Y +50, LD2450 at Y −50, mic between, SCD41 on the rear face.
  7. XL4015: trim to 6.0 V with a multimeter, unloaded, then wire to PCA9685 V+. Nothing else touches this rail. Charge the pack on a concrete or tile surface, never on carpet or a bench full of parts — there is no fire bag.
  8. Wiring, in this order: motor leads to the four sockets (X = FL, Y = FR, Z = RL, A = RR) — the shield sockets are 4-pin 2.54 mm male headers, so the motor leads need 4-pin Dupont housings or stepper cables; pack T-plug pigtail split with Wago 221s (or solder) to shield VMOT, XL4015 IN and LM2596 IN; LM2596 trimmed to 5.0 V feeds the fan and both radars' 5 V — NOT the shield header or the ESP32 pin; Pi to R4 by USB, Pi to ESP32 by USB, Pi to webcam by USB; I2C from the Pi to PCA9685, both ToF (with XSHUT lines), SCD41; the thermal array on the hardware second bus; radars' UART to the ESP32; mic on I2S. 1000 µF across the PCA9685 V+ terminal. Tie everything down. Run a dedicated ground wire from the LM2596 output GND to the ESP32's GND pin alongside the radar signal wires — otherwise the radar UART's ground reference travels through two USB cables.
  9. Wheels-up test before the floor: each wheel forward and back in turn, then all four. A wheel that hums but does not turn is a stalled driver — check Vref and the MS3 cap. Then the floor: forward, reverse, strafe, spin, stop. Then ten minutes at cruise with a finger on each heatsink.
  10. Firmware before the floor: the R4 must ramp every speed change over ~0.3 s — a stepper commanded from rest straight to 3,800 steps/s skips and stalls; the pan lock means heading comes from wheel odometry — commanded heading, which mecanum slip makes coarse, so a radar bearing from rotation is ±20° at best; the tilt never goes below the Ceres mechanical floor; a dropped serial link stops the motors within 200 ms.
  11. The torque test: wheels up, lever on one shaft, hanging scale on the lever, drive at 24 rpm and load it until it skips. T = F × arm. Compare with 0.254 N·m. This retires the biggest assumption in the design.
  12. Sensors: one radar powered at a time first, then both, then a person walking, standing, lying behind a door. Thermal and camera on a walking person from 4 m. CO2 in a closed room over ten minutes. Mic with the motors off.

Caught in review

Mistakes found and fixed while checking this design, kept here so the reasoning survives.

  • Microstepping jumpers sit under the driver sockets — caps, not wires, and they go on before the drivers. I first said Dupont leads would do; you caught it.
  • Four screw lengths I specified were in no kit on the list: M5×10, M5×20, M4×16 with nyloc, M3×30. Fixed with counterbores in the printed parts so the corner kit's M5×8 and the T-nut kit's M4×12 reach, the cradle and turret moved to M3 from the M3 kit, and the fan to cable ties. Zero purchases added.
  • When the 5 V buck left the list, the fan and the radars lost their 5 V source. Both now run on USB pass-through from the Pi, with the Pi's 1.2 A USB budget written out above.
  • Motor lead length has never been documented. The far-corner motor needs about 400 mm to reach the shield; plan to extend with your 22 AWG and crimp Dupont housings.
  • The design mass crept from 4.84 to 5.41 kg through three honest corrections — brackets at 113 g not 60, a 162 g webcam, the power bank — and failed the ramp at 1.5 A before the 2200 mAh pack brought it back to 5.11 kg. Every gram from here spends margin.
  • The frame drawing had two corner brackets per joint 'one top, one bottom'. A 2020 inner corner holds exactly one 20 mm bracket, and one on the top faces would sit under the deck. Fixed: four brackets, deck tiles as the second shear plane.
  • The deck tiles were drawn 350 mm wide with six M5s 'into the side-rail top slots'. A 350 mm tile reaches the side rails' inner faces at Y ±175 — the top slots are at Y ±185. It could never have been bolted down. Fixed: 390 mm tiles.
  • The build order assembled the frame before the motor brackets. The kits' T-nuts are sliding, not drop-in, and a butt-jointed frame closes every slot end. Fixed: every T-nut goes in before the corners close.
  • The thermal array was put on a bit-banged i2c-gpio bus 'at 1 MHz'. Software I2C on a Pi manages ~100–200 kHz; the MLX90640 would have run at ~3 fps. Fixed: hardware second bus (i2c3 on a Pi 4) at 400 kHz.
  • The Pi's USB budget counted the webcam at 250 mA. A C920 draws ~500 mA at 1080p and the R4 WiFi's radio ~150 mA; with fan and radars on USB pass-through it was over 1.2 A. Fixed: fan and radars on an LM2596 at 5.0 V, R4 radio off.
  • The turret is inside the radar beam — 270 mm ahead, 60 mm below boresight. Not a wiring fix: the radars are read only with the turret parked, and LD2450 targets under 0.5 m are discarded.
  • Nothing on the list could split the T-plug into three branches, hold a coupler on a shaft, or connect motor leads to the shield's 4-pin headers. Added a parts-box-first consumables group: threadlocker, Wago 221s, stepper cables, a 1000 µF cap for the servo rail, a plastic trimmer tool.
  • Two 390 mm deck tiles fit no common print bed. Now four 170 × 195 quadrants with tongue-and-rebate seams.
  • The wheels sat 25 mm outboard of the bracket legs (a fudge in the formula). Every millimetre multiplies the motor-bearing load; now 5 mm, and the bearing check uses the real overhang: indoor floors pass, a 20 mm threshold does not.
  • The Ceres pan servo is continuous-rotation — it cannot hold or report an angle, so the camera would have had no known bearing. Pan is now the rover rotating on its wheels with odometry; the pan stage is locked; tilt keeps its mechanical floor.
  • The radar bar called for heat-set inserts that nothing on the list supplies. Through-bolted with M3 + nuts.
  • The bracket's bundled screws are probably M3×8: through a 3 mm leg into a ~4.5 mm flange hole they bottom out and never clamp. Use M3×6 from the kit.
  • The sense-resistor code on the DRV8825 carriers decides Vref; assuming R100 on an R220 board gives 0.68 A and a rover that stalls. Read the code first.
  • Person detection was quoted at 25 fps; a Pi 4 running TFLite MobileNet-SSD at 300 px does ~10 fps. Corrected.
  • The LD2450 shares the 24 GHz band with the LD2410C 100 mm away and their interference is untested. It now defaults off.
  • The front ToF at 81 mm sees the floor beyond ~0.34 m through a 27° cone. ROI narrowed or clip tilted up.
  • The kit ships exactly four drivers. The owned TB6600 is now the documented emergency fourth axis.
  • Nothing said to unplug the pack when parked: idle draw from three regulators kills a LiPo overnight.
  • The deck tiles were 170 long; the cross-rail top slot sits at X ±165 and a 9 mm M5 counterbore centred there needs 4.5 mm of tile beyond it. It had 0.5 mm. Tiles are now 175.
  • Power-up order was never stated. VMOT with the R4 unpowered leaves every DRV8825 enabled at full holding current. Logic first, pack second; reverse to shut down.
  • The LD2410C's nearest gates look straight at the turret's metal. Unmasked, it reports a stationary person for the life of the rover. Gates 0–1 are now set to ignore.
  • Deck screws were specified at the corner brackets' 3.0 N·m, into 3 mm of PLA under the head. 1.5 N·m.
  • VL53L1X modules powered at 5 V would put 5 V pull-ups on Pi GPIO pins. 3.3 V.
  • The XT60 disconnect was under the deck. It is now brought up to the rear edge as a one-handed emergency stop.
  • The optional SCD41's I2C tops out at 100 kHz; fitted, it slows the shared bus. Stated.
  • Every under-deck wire needs a way through a full-coverage deck. Pass-through slots added.
  • The SCD41 sat on the post's rear face, which became the open side of the flat-printed U-channel. Moved to a side face.
  • Fastener counts were stale after the tile change (30 M5, not 22; 24 M3). Corrected; still inside both kits.
  • Two USB serial devices with the same /dev/ttyACM naming swap order between boots. Now addressed by serial-by-id; otherwise the first boot on stage sends step commands to the radar bridge.
  • The mecanum X/O rule is stated both ways by different vendors. Replaced with a drive-forward test that cannot be misread.

  • The second LM2596, the Wago block and the top-side T-plug e-stop were wired in the text and placed nowhere. Now placed: LM2596 outboard of the XL4015, Wago and the e-stop connector at the rear-right corner, clear of the Pi and the post flange.
